# Memo: a sector-pair book versus the Russell 1000 Growth

**Educational note on a study ending 30 September 2026. Not a recommendation to buy or sell any security. Figures are the Yahoo Finance cache dated 9 October 2026.**

**The book.** At each quarter-end from 30 September 2021 through 30 June 2026, hold the two largest companies in each of seven sectors in a fixed 77-name U.S. large-cap growth list. Equal-weight the 14 names. Let the weights drift until the next quarter-end. The last holding period ends 30 September 2026. The benchmark proxy is IWF, the iShares Russell 1000 Growth ETF.

**The result.** The book compounded at **20.8%** a year. IWF compounded at **13.5%**. A cap-weighted portfolio of the same 77 names compounded at **17.9%**, and an equal-weighted portfolio of those names also compounded at **17.9%** (total returns 127.3% and 128.1%). The book beat IWF in 14 of 20 quarters and beat the cap-weighted list in 9 of 20. The average quarterly gap versus the cap-weighted list is 0.4 percentage points, with a descriptive t-statistic of 0.5. The book also had the shallower drawdown: −19.9%, against −30.7% for IWF. In calendar 2022 the book was −16.2% and IWF was −29.3%.

**What the weights explain, and what they do not.** Brinson-Fachler attribution against the cap-weighted 77 is an identity. Over the full window the cumulative returns are 156.7% for the book and 127.3% for the cap-weighted list, a gap of 29.4 percentage points. Linked allocation is **−16.7** points, selection **−1.0**, interaction **+47.1**. Sector bets, taken alone, subtracted. The book averaged 14% in technology against 47% for the cap-weighted list, and technology's linked total is −34 points. NVIDIA is in the book for 6 of 20 rebalances, first on 31 December 2024. Underweighting NVIDIA is the largest single name effect, about −21 points. Overweighting Caterpillar and Eli Lilly are the largest positive name effects, about +17 and +15 points. Industrials, consumer cyclical, and health care are where the book earned the gap back.

**Versus IWF, half the story is the list.** Cumulative returns are 156.7% versus 88.2%. Of that 68.5 point gap, about 42 points in the linked split is the cap-weighted 77 beating IWF. Free data has no history of Russell 1000 Growth membership, so that piece is not a stock-level attribution. The 77-name basket still tracks IWF closely (correlation 0.99, beta 0.99, tracking error 2.9% a year) and finishes ahead. Every name in the file was still a large cap in October 2026. IWF held the live ETF the whole time.

**Style spreads do not eat the average.** The book's beta to IWF is 0.79, so it was not a leveraged copy of the ETF. A regression of the monthly gap versus IWF on four ETF spreads (Russell 1000 Value minus Growth, Russell 2000 minus Russell 1000, a momentum ETF minus the S&P 500, a quality ETF minus the S&P 500) has an in-sample R² of 0.52 and an out-of-sample R² of 0.42. The one solid loading is value-minus-growth, at 0.37. Value lagged growth in this window, so that loading reduced the average gap. The intercept is about 6.5 percentage points a year. The spreads describe the month-to-month path. They do not account for the mean.

**What this is not.** It is not a claim that the rule will keep working. Twenty quarters is a short sample, the t-statistic versus the cap-weighted list is 0.5, and there are no transaction costs in the base figures (a 10 basis point one-way cost would have taken about 0.04 points off the CAGR at the turnover this book actually had). IWF is an ETF, not the index. The market-cap ranks use a point-in-time share count aligned to Yahoo's split-adjusted prices, and they match Yahoo's own market cap at the end of the file within a few percent. They are not official index weights.
